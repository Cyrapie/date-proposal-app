There's a precise moment where a first date is won or lost, and it isn't
the date itself. It's the exchange of messages that comes before it.

## The problem with "whenever works for you"

"Whenever works for you" is a courtesy that turns into a dead end. The
other person has to guess your availability, suggest a day, get turned
down, start over. By the third back-and-forth, the momentum is gone.

The opposite works better: **propose first, let them choose after.**

## Three places, maximum

Beyond three, it stops being a choice and becomes a questionnaire. Two or
three addresses are enough, and they say something about you: what you
like, the neighbourhood you know, the level of formality you're picturing.

One tip: mix the registers. A wine bar and a neighbourhood restaurant don't
set up the same kind of evening. Leaving that choice open lets the other
person set the tempo.

## Two time slots, not ten

The temptation is to offer a wide range to maximise the odds. That's a
mistake. Ten time slots make your schedule look empty, and worse, they
force the other person to do the sorting.

Two well-chosen slots — one on a weekday, one on the weekend — cover most
situations.

## The note matters more than anything else

An invitation with no message is just a booking. Two lines are enough:
what made you think of this person, or why this particular place. That's
the part that gets reread.

> Everything else is just logistics. And logistics can be automated.

## And afterward

Once the answer comes in, the date needs to exist somewhere. An `.ics`
file in both calendars, and the matter is settled. No one asks "what time
was it again?" the night before.
